\section*{Abstract}\label{sec:abstract}

\setlength{\emergencystretch}{2em}

IoT workflows can connect sensor events to physical or simulated actions, but a
language-model proposal is not necessarily contract-valid or authorized. The
aim was to design, implement, and evaluate the Obid contribution: a bounded
single-agent decision and reliability layer that extends an inherited
Yacoub-compatible n8n workflow-to-action boundary while preserving the shared
action interface. The layer combined two read-only tools, bounded memory,
runtime validation, deterministic policy, and executable Human-in-the-Loop
(HITL) control.
\texttt{CONFIG-OBID} and the inherited minimal-agent baseline,
\texttt{CONFIG-BASELINE}, were compared under a frozen repeated protocol in one
temperature-to-fan scenario; every scheduled primary attempt, including
failures and deviations, was retained.

The evaluation retained 85 primary attempts. \texttt{CONFIG-OBID} produced the
expected outcome in 35/35 RQ1 attempts. Runtime validation blocked all five
injected invalid actions, and all ten pending HITL trials held the action
without release. Assigned approvals were 5/5; assigned denials were 4/5 because
one assigned denial received an actual human approval and remained a protocol
deviation. Zero invalid or unapproved actions crossed the shared interface in
the observed trials. Relative to \texttt{CONFIG-BASELINE}, reliability
differences occurred in malformed-input handling and duplicate-action
suppression, while \texttt{CONFIG-OBID} had higher median automated latency in
all three measured families. These results show that the bounded extension
combined expected decision behavior with executable release controls in the
evaluated setting, but do not establish universal reliability or production
safety. The evidence is limited to one model, one temperature/fan scenario,
five repetitions per cell, and a simulated fan boundary.

Keywords: n8n, agentic workflows, Internet of Things, runtime validation,
Human-in-the-Loop, workflow automation
