\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}
\setlength{\emergencystretch}{4em}

This chapter interprets the observations reported in Chapter~\ref{sec:results}
within the study design described in Chapter~\ref{sec:method}. The discussion
begins with the principal evidence boundaries because the positive observations
only have meaning within those boundaries. It then considers the three research
questions, the reliability--latency tradeoff, the role of the extended
configuration, and the implications of placing executable controls between a
model proposal and the shared action interface. The final sections distinguish
inherited and Obid-created work, assess validity threats, and identify broader
conceptual relevance, ethical concerns, and evidence-generating future work.

\subsection{Limitations First}\label{subsec:discussion-limitations-first}

The evaluation was deliberately narrow. It studied one temperature-to-fan
scenario with one simulated fan target under a fixed threshold rule, using one stored model
identifier and one bounded-memory configuration. This creates a controlled
comparison, but it restricts any claim about other sensors, actions, devices,
models, or operational environments. In particular, the work did not conduct a
new physical Obid Raspberry Pi experiment and did not test production safety,
deployment scale, or recovery from device and network failures.

The repeated evidence is also descriptive. Each applicable case/configuration
cell contains five attempts from one execution period. Those repetitions reveal
whether the same frozen behavior recurred during that period, but they do not
support significance testing, confidence intervals, population estimates, or a
claim that future provider behavior will be identical. The comparison likewise
contains no second model, alternative memory strategy, multi-agent
configuration, or additional device against which the observed behavior could
be generalized.

Several safety and input cases entered through controlled seams. The malformed
family represented one missing-\texttt{value} form, the invalid-action family
injected one unsupported \texttt{fan\_reverse} candidate, and the HITL family
supplied one exact approval-required policy context. These cases provide direct
evidence about the exercised validation and release paths. They do not show
that the model naturally generated every fault, that the system autonomously
identified arbitrary risk, or that all malformed and hostile inputs would be
contained. State-dependent behavior was similarly limited to one
A$\rightarrow$B$\rightarrow$C sequence.

they restrict. The limitations are not all defects. Some, such as controlled
injection and a narrow action vocabulary, improve attribution inside the test,
while reducing the range of situations to which the result can be transferred.

The bounded memory was process-local and non-durable. Its active window exposed
two completed interactions, but a process restart could remove that state, and
the stored state represented recent decision context rather than an independent
confirmation that a physical actuator had executed. Latency was measured across
complete configurations with unequal workloads, so the data cannot isolate the
time attributable to the model, tools, memory, validation, policy, or routing.
The HITL durations are even more context-dependent: human waiting came from ten
controlled sessions, with six actual approvals, four actual denials, and one
departure from the assigned decision. Finally, one positive model-call record
lacked token telemetry and direct cost telemetry was unavailable. These gaps
limit resource and cost interpretation without invalidating the retained
observable action evidence.

\subsection{Interpretation of RQ1}\label{subsec:discussion-rq1}

\noindent\textbf{RQ1:} How accurately and consistently does the extended
n8n-based agentic decision layer produce the expected IoT action across defined
normal, malformed, and state-dependent test cases?

Within the frozen cases, CONFIG-OBID produced the expected observable outcome
in all 35 of 35 attempts. High, Low, Threshold, Malformed, and Memory A, B, and C
each produced 5/5 correct attempts, as reported in
Table~\ref{tab:results-rq1}. The inclusive threshold boundary remained intact:
an input of $30.0\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ led to the expected \texttt{fan\_on}
action. The malformed event terminated without a shared action. In the memory
sequence, A and C produced the required state-changing actions, while B kept
the already-on state without issuing a duplicate action.

Modal agreement was 100\% in every family. In these observations, repeated
sameness therefore aligned with correctness: the most common normalized
outcome was also the frozen expected outcome in every family. That alignment is
important because consistency alone is not a quality measure. A system can
repeat the same incorrect action, as the baseline Memory-B observations later
demonstrate.

The evidence therefore supports only a narrow interpretation: CONFIG-OBID
behaved accurately and consistently across the observed repetitions of the exact normal,
malformed, and state-dependent cases. It is not evidence of universal agent
reliability. Only one malformed form and one bounded state sequence were tested,
and five repetitions per family remain a small descriptive sample. The reason
field was checked as a non-empty, schema-valid string, not scored for exact
wording or broader semantic quality. No alternative memory strategy or IoT
domain was evaluated, so RQ1 cannot support claims about memory-design
superiority, durable state, or behavior beyond the frozen interface.

\subsection{Interpretation of RQ2}\label{subsec:discussion-rq2}

\noindent\textbf{RQ2:} How effectively do runtime structured-output validation,
action policies, and Human-in-the-Loop approval prevent invalid or risky agent
actions from reaching the shared IoT action interface?

The controlled RQ2 trials distinguish three layers of release control. First,
the contract-invalid \texttt{fan\_reverse} candidate was blocked in 5/5 attempts
with \texttt{UNKNOWN\_ACTION}. Policy did not release an action, neither fan
endpoint was called, and the simulated fan remained off. The candidate was
injected after the agent boundary, so this result concerns the actual runtime
validator rather than the probability that a model would naturally invent that
particular action. It also shows that approval was not a rescue path for an
invalid proposal: the proposal stopped before HITL.

Second, all ten approval-required trials were observed while genuinely pending.
Each pending snapshot contained the held action, a null released action, zero
calls to both action endpoints, and an off fan state. The evidence therefore
supports a practical distinction between displaying an action for review and
authorizing it to cross the interface. A schema-valid proposal had not become an
executable action merely because it had reached the review stage.

Third, actual human input determined what happened after the gate. The five
assigned approval attempts were correct: release occurred only after approval,
and the unchanged held \texttt{fan\_on} action reached \texttt{/fan/on}. Four of
the five assigned denial attempts received an actual denial and released
nothing. Assigned denial correctness was therefore 4/5, not 5/5. Across the ten
trials, the plan contained five approvals and five denials, while the submitted
decisions contained six approvals and four denials. This difference was retained
as one controlled-human-decision protocol deviation.

\paragraph{Denial R03.}
Run \texttt{\seqsplit{S10\_EVAL-HITL-01B\_CONFIG-OBID\_R03}} was assigned a
denial but received an actual approval. Before that input, its action remained
held, its release was null, neither endpoint had been called, and the fan was
off. After the approval, the same valid action was released unchanged and
reached \texttt{/fan/on}. The run remains incorrect against the assigned denial
oracle; assigned denial remains 4/5, and the total number of incorrect primary
outcomes remains 11. At runtime, however, the release followed an actual
approval. It was neither invalid nor unapproved, and it must not be described as
a successful denial, a validator failure, a policy failure, or a HITL semantic
failure.

This distinction explains why the locked historical processed crossing flag of
\texttt{1} and the final interpretation of zero invalid or unapproved
crossings can coexist. The append-only correction preserves the raw record, the
original processed output, the assigned oracle, and the protocol deviation. It
supersedes only the final crossing interpretation. Rewriting the decision or
discarding the attempt would have made the assigned-denial aggregate look
cleaner while weakening traceability. Retention instead exposes two different
questions: whether the planned test instruction was followed, and whether the
runtime released an action without the authorization it actually received.

The RQ2 observations therefore support the effectiveness of the exercised
contract validation, deterministic policy, pending hold, and human release gate
within the frozen action interface. They do not prove production safety. Only
one unsupported action form and one controlled policy context were used, and
the system did not autonomously discover arbitrary risk. Replay protection,
authentication, timeouts, idempotency, cryptographic integrity, and broad
adversarial behavior were not evaluated.

\subsection{Interpretation of RQ3}\label{subsec:discussion-rq3}

\noindent\textbf{RQ3:} What reliability and latency differences are observed
between the inherited minimal agent baseline and the extended Obid agentic
workflow under the same defined IoT test cases?

CONFIG-BASELINE and CONFIG-OBID agreed in the ordinary High, Low, and Threshold
families: both configurations produced 5/5 correct outcomes in each. They also
agreed on the state-changing actions in Memory A and Memory C, again at 5/5.
The comparison therefore does not suggest that the extended configuration
improved every tested event. For direct threshold-driven state changes, the
inherited minimal agent already produced the expected observable actions across
the observed repetitions.

The configurations differed in the Malformed and Memory-B families. The
baseline was correct in 0/5 malformed attempts, compared with 5/5 for Obid. Its
malformed outcomes had 60\% modal agreement because three attempts routed an
incorrect \texttt{fan\_off} action while two ended with non-success statuses.
For Memory B, the baseline issued a duplicate \texttt{fan\_on} action in all
five attempts. It consequently had 100\% modal agreement but 0/5 correctness,
whereas Obid produced the expected no-action outcome in 5/5. The scored failure
was the duplicate observable action, not the mere fact that the baseline was
configured without memory. These results reinforce that modal agreement measures
repetition, not correctness.

The automated latency comparison covered only High, Low, and Threshold. Median
duration was 2130~ms for the baseline and 3792~ms for Obid in High, 2105~ms and
4472~ms in Low, and 2083~ms and 4487~ms in Threshold. CONFIG-OBID therefore had
the higher median in all three measured families. All five observations in each
cell were retained, including the longer values; none was removed as an outlier.
These medians describe complete configuration paths, not isolated component
timings. With five observations per cell, they support no significance or
population-level performance claim.

associated with the extended decision and reliability path as a complete

Whether this tradeoff is acceptable depends on the application. A bounded

deadline and simpler controls. The present data cannot
resolve that design decision outside the simulated fan scenario, and it provides
no basis for an economic cost comparison.

\subsection{Role of Tools and Bounded Memory}\label{subsec:discussion-tools-memory}

The readiness evidence showed that the permitted tool-use cycle could execute:
cold sessions could obtain the threshold and current status, while recent state
could replace an unnecessary status lookup. In the repeated evaluation,
Memory~B specifically required the already-on state to be recognized and a
duplicate action to be suppressed. CONFIG-OBID produced that no-action outcome
in all five attempts, while CONFIG-BASELINE issued duplicate \texttt{fan\_on}
in all five.

The evidence is consistent with the intended combined role of controlled tool
use, explicit state representation, and bounded memory. It does not identify
which of those elements caused the difference. No ablation removed the tools or
memory independently, and the two configurations also differed in prompts,
input handling, validation, policy, and routing. The study therefore cannot
claim that memory alone produced Memory-B correctness, that another memory
design would perform worse, or that the agent had broad planning ability.

The memory boundary is also operationally important. Two completed interactions
were visible within a synthetic session, but the state was process-local rather
than restart-durable. It supported one short controlled sequence; it did not
replace device confirmation, persistent storage, or a general history service.

\subsection{Significance of Runtime Validation and HITL}\label{subsec:discussion-safety}

The safety results illustrate the difference between prompt instructions and
executable release controls. A prompt can request a permitted action and valid
shape, but the model output remained a candidate. Contract-specific validation
ran before release, deterministic policy selected allow, block, or approval
handling, and a valid approval-required action remained held until a human
decision. An invalid action stopped before HITL, so an approval could not turn
\texttt{fan\_reverse} into an allowed action. For a valid held action, approval
released the unchanged four-field object and denial released nothing.

Within the frozen action interface, this arrangement moved final release
authority outside the model. The practical value is not that the model becomes
incapable of error, but that a proposal must pass observable, deterministic
conditions before the runtime can invoke an action endpoint. The R03 deviation further
shows why the human cannot be treated as a deterministic software component:
the system followed the actual approval, while the experiment remained
incorrect against its assigned denial.

The ten segmented HITL records add a separate timing implication. Human waiting
was the largest segment in every observed trial. HITL therefore changes the
completion model from purely automated processing to a human-dependent one.
Response time can vary with the person, interface, workload, and context, so
these session-specific durations are not a general usability study. They were
correctly excluded from the automated RQ3 latency comparison; mixing them with
automated duration would obscure both the configuration cost and the human
oversight cost.

An earlier child-Wait placement failed closed during readiness work and was
replaced before the frozen experiment. That development evidence is relevant
because a configured review node alone did not guarantee that approval
propagated to the caller. It is not an additional experimental result. The
final observation concerns the repaired, frozen topology and its executable
pending/release behavior.

These controls remain contract-specific. The study did not evaluate generic
cybersecurity, arbitrary adversarial input, cryptographic integrity,
authentication, replay, timeout handling, or complete failure prevention. The
controlled policy context supplied risk rather than discovering it
autonomously. Zero observed invalid or unapproved crossings should therefore be
read as a result of the exercised trials, not as a production-safety guarantee.

\subsection{Inherited and Obid Attribution}\label{subsec:discussion-provenance}

The comparison integrated inherited and newly created work without changing
authorship. The middleware and action infrastructure, threshold and action
semantics, minimal-agent baseline, deterministic anchor, and earlier Raspberry
Pi/action-side context remain \texttt{YACOUB\_INHERITED}; the prior physical
material is used here only as \texttt{REFERENCE\_ONLY}. The sensor and action
contracts and compatible endpoint meanings remain the
\texttt{SHARED\_INTERFACE}. Reproducing and measuring those artifacts did not
make them an Obid implementation contribution.

The stronger single Decision Agent configuration, two read-only tools, bounded
memory, internal decision envelope, executable runtime validation,
deterministic policy and HITL, controlled seams, evaluation protocol, and
evidence processing are \texttt{OBID\_CREATED}. Earlier Yacoub safety and HITL
specification concepts remain inherited reference context. Step 10 also
produced new Obid evaluation evidence for
both configurations, including the raw records, summaries, traceability, and
append-only R03 correction. Evaluation ownership and implementation ownership
are therefore different: the observations of baseline weaknesses are new
evidence, but the evaluated baseline remains inherited.

This distinction limits the contribution claim in two directions. Obid should
not claim the middleware, shared action contract, baseline, or prior physical
action-side work. Conversely, the repeated comparison and executable
validation/policy/HITL evidence concern the Obid study, not Yacoub's earlier
evaluation. The deterministic anchor provides context for compatibility and
does not replace CONFIG-BASELINE as the RQ3 comparator.

\subsection{Threats to Validity}\label{subsec:discussion-validity}

The main threats arise from when and how the runs were executed, what the frozen
oracle represents, and how narrowly the configuration space was sampled.
Table~\ref{tab:discussion-validity} pairs each category with the controls that
were actually used and the risk that remains. The controls reduce uncertainty;
they do not eliminate it.

For internal validity, the deterministic five-round rotation balanced ordinal
positions but was not randomization. Pairing the configurations, alternating
which ran first, resetting state, and isolating memory sessions reduced obvious
order and carry-over effects. Provider conditions could still change during the
execution period, process-local state could behave differently after a restart,
and manual approval remained variable. Retaining interruptions, failed attempts,
and the R03 deviation rather than substituting successful reruns prevents those
events from being hidden, but does not make them disappear.

Construct validity depends on the frozen observable oracle. It captures action
presence, contractual fields, terminal stage, endpoint use, and simulated final
state, which fit the research questions. It does not score hidden reasoning or
the semantic quality of natural-language reasons beyond schema validity.
Malformed input bypassed normal transport normalization at comparable seams,
\texttt{fan\_reverse} was injected rather than generated naturally, and policy
risk was supplied in a controlled context. Complete-configuration latency also
bundles unequal work. These choices improve control over specific constructs
while narrowing what those constructs mean.

External and statistical limits reinforce each other. A single small case matrix
and five attempts per cell cannot establish behavior across other models,
memory designs, action vocabularies, physical devices, execution dates, or human
populations. Reproducibility is stronger for repository artifacts than for the
external service: versions, options, exports, manifests, raw records, and
traceability were frozen, but provider implementation details, private
credential attachment, and unstored defaults remain outside the repository.
The appropriate standard is bounded traceability, not a claim of perfect
reproducibility.

\subsection{Broader IoT Relevance}\label{subsec:discussion-relevance}

The scenario can be read as a small event--state--action proxy for contexts such
as building ventilation, bounded industrial monitoring, environmental sensing,
health or environmental monitoring workflows, and facility or laboratory
automation. What may transfer conceptually is the control pattern, not the
measured performance:

\begin{center}
\small
event $\rightarrow$ controlled decision $\rightarrow$ contract validation\\
$\rightarrow$ deterministic policy $\rightarrow$ optional human approval
$\rightarrow$ approved action interface
\end{center}

None of those wider contexts was implemented. Each would need its own event and
action contracts, allowed vocabulary, risk policy, approval responsibilities,
and regulatory analysis. A health-related or industrial action may have much
greater consequences than switching a simulated fan. Physical-device behavior,
security and access control, concurrency and scale, communication failure, and
recovery would therefore require direct testing before the pattern could be
used operationally.

The narrow shared action interface illustrates one potentially useful design
principle: keep the model-facing decision space separate from a small,
validated actuator vocabulary. A second is to make hold and release states
observable so that approval can be audited. These are design implications from
the prototype and its frozen evidence, not empirical proof that the same pattern
will succeed in another sector.

\subsection{Ethical and Societal Considerations}\label{subsec:discussion-ethics}

AI-mediated control raises responsibility questions even when the action is as
limited as a simulated fan. An operator should be able to see what action is
proposed, why it is within the allowed vocabulary, whether policy requires
approval, and what decision released it. Moving release authority outside the
model can support accountability only if the validation and policy are themselves
understandable, maintained, and appropriate for the domain.

Human oversight also creates risks. A reviewer may approve too quickly because
the system appears reliable, misunderstand the consequence, or become fatigued
by repeated requests. Conversely, a slow response may make an otherwise safe
action ineffective. R03 gives a concrete, bounded example of responsibility
when human input differs from the planned procedure: the runtime followed the
actual approval, while the evidence preserved that the assigned denial was not
performed. A real deployment would need clear authority, escalation, timeout,
and accountability rules rather than assuming that a human presence alone
solves automation risk.

The study applied privacy minimization to its evidence. No personal reviewer
identity was needed, credential values and transient private links were
excluded, and the extracted execution view was bounded to required evidence.
These choices reduce unnecessary exposure but do not establish secure access
control. A production system would still need authentication, authorization,
credential management, integrity protection, and attention to potentially
sensitive sensor data.

Finally, consequences rise with the domain. An incorrect ventilation command
may cause discomfort or wasted energy; an incorrect industrial or health-related
command can threaten equipment or people. Narrow action contracts, explicit
approval, auditability, and retention of failures are therefore ethically
relevant, but the prototype does not resolve automation bias, human workload,
security, or responsibility. Hiding failed and unexpected trials would make
those concerns harder to assess; retaining them supports more honest oversight.

\subsection{Future Work}\label{subsec:discussion-future}

Future work should generate evidence for the limitations identified above,
rather than add features without a corresponding evaluation. Every direction
below changes either the evaluated implementation or the frozen protocol and
would therefore require an explicit reopening of the implementation/evidence
freeze before new observations could be reported.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Larger and multi-date repetitions.} The motivating limitation is
five observations per cell from one execution period. The implementation could
remain fixed, but the protocol and schedule would need additional repetitions
on separate dates. New raw records should support uncertainty and temporal
stability analysis chosen in advance. The evidence freeze would need to reopen
for a new evaluation campaign.

\item \textbf{Broader malformed and unsupported-action families.} The current
evidence covers one missing field and one injected \texttt{fan\_reverse}
candidate. The case manifest, controlled seams, and oracles should be extended
to additional missing, extra, mistyped, malformed-JSON, target, and action
conditions. Evidence would need to show validator terminals, interface crossing,
endpoint counts, and state for every new family. Both protocol and evidence
freezes would need to reopen.

\item \textbf{Additional policies and HITL contexts.} One exact policy context
cannot represent different risk levels or approval responsibilities. This work
would add explicit deterministic policies and corresponding review scenarios,
then collect pending, approval, denial, timeout, and deviation evidence for each.
Because policy semantics and evaluated behavior would change, the implementation
and evidence freezes would both need to reopen.

\item \textbf{Stronger release-channel controls.} Authentication, replay
protection, idempotency, timeouts, and stronger action integrity were outside the
prototype. Implementing them would change the middleware-facing release path.
New negative, retry, duplicate-request, expired-approval, and tamper tests would
be required, including recovery observations. This is a substantive freeze
reopening, not a documentation-only extension.

\item \textbf{Physical hardware and multiple targets.} The simulated fan does
not establish physical actuation, device feedback, or multi-device routing.
Future implementation could connect approved actions to instrumented hardware
and add separately contracted targets. Evidence should include command receipt,
measured physical state, communication failures, safe recovery, and target
isolation. Hardware and new contracts require reopening both freezes.

\item \textbf{Durable or alternative bounded memory.} The current memory is one
process-local two-interaction strategy. A comparison could introduce restart-
durable state and at least one alternative bounded strategy while preserving
common stimuli. Evidence should cover restart behavior, stale-state handling,
duplicate suppression, contamination between sessions, and latency. Any new
state component and comparison protocol require reopening the freezes.

\item \textbf{Additional model configurations.} One stored model configuration
cannot show model sensitivity. A controlled comparison should keep contracts,
prompts where compatible, cases, release controls, order, and timing boundaries
fixed while varying a declared model configuration. New reliability, safety,
latency, and telemetry records would be needed. The model/runtime and evidence
identities would change, so the freeze must reopen.

\item \textbf{Deferred validator-agent comparison.} The optional second-agent
configuration remains \texttt{DEFERRED\_AFTER\_FINAL\_FREEZE} and was not
implemented. A future supplementary configuration could compare it with the
deterministic runtime validator without replacing the core system. It would
require a new architecture identity, matched cases, explicit failure and latency
measures, and provenance records. Implementing or evaluating it requires an
explicit freeze reopening.

\item \textbf{Richer resource and cost telemetry.} One positive-call token record
was unavailable and direct cost telemetry was absent. Future orchestration could
capture provider-approved token, request, retry, resource, and billed-cost fields
without estimating missing values. Completeness checks and traceability to each
primary run would be required. Instrumentation and the evidence schema would
change, so a new freeze would be necessary.

\item \textbf{Human-factors evaluation.} Ten controlled sessions do not measure
usability, workload, comprehension, or population response time. An appropriate
study would need a reviewer interface, participant and ethics procedures,
defined tasks, decision-quality measures, workload/usability instruments, and
separate human and automated timing. The resulting evidence would constitute a
new study and would require explicit reopening of the relevant implementation,
protocol, and evidence freezes.
\end{enumerate}

These directions preserve the central lesson of the current boundary: a new
feature becomes a supported thesis claim only when its behavior is evaluated
under a declared protocol and retained evidence. Until such work is performed,
the validator-agent, additional models and memories, physical devices, stronger
security controls, and broader human study remain future work rather than
current contributions.
